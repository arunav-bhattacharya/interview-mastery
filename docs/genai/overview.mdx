## The 70/30 rule

Most "learn AI" plans flip the ratio — 70% reading, 30% building. That's how you end up with a head full of vocabulary and no working systems. This curriculum inverts it: you spend the bulk of your hours typing code into a working LLM and watching it fail, then reading just enough to understand *why* it failed.

The weekly budget — for one slow, sustainable week, not a sprint:

| Block | Hours | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Reading / watching | 2 | One video, one article, maybe a course module |
| Building | 4 | Hands-on code: scripts, agents, pipelines |
| Writing | 2 | README, architecture notes, decision log |
| Buffer / review | 2 | Debugging, re-running, refactoring |

## Recommended loop per topic

1. **15 min — Read the intro.** Just enough to know what the thing is.
2. **30–45 min — Watch the canonical video at 1.5×.** Karpathy, Hamel, Chase — they're worth your time even at full speed.
3. **45 min — Build a minimal demonstration.** No framework. Just the smallest possible thing that exhibits the concept.
4. **45 min — Rebuild it with a framework** (LangChain / LangGraph / etc.). Notice what got easier and what got hidden.
5. **15 min — Document.** Three bullet points in the repo README about what you learned and where it'd break in production.

A few JVM-to-Python pitfalls worth knowing before you write your first script:

- **Mutable default arguments** are a footgun: `def f(x=[])` shares that list across calls. Use `None` and create inside.
- **`self` is explicit** in every method signature. Forgetting it is the most common compile-equivalent error.
- **`asyncio` is cooperative**, not preemptive — `await` is the only place your function can be paused. Don't expect Java's executor semantics.
- **`pyproject.toml`** is the equivalent of `pom.xml` or `build.gradle`. Combined with `uv`, you get reproducible installs.
- **Type hints are advisory** by default — they don't enforce anything until you run `mypy` / `pyright`. Useful, but don't trust them to catch runtime bugs.

## Anti-patterns to dodge

- **Tutorial dependency.** Finishing courses isn't progress. Shipping something — even a 50-line script — is progress.
- **Framework hopping.** Three weeks of "LangChain vs LlamaIndex vs Haystack" debates produces zero working code.
- **Chasing every model release.** Most of your tradeoffs are about retrieval, prompting and orchestration. The model is a knob, not the system.
- **Backend-rigor too early.** When you're learning, your agent code will be ugly — long prompts, branchy logic, half-baked retries. Resist the urge to refactor it into Clean Architecture before you have evals to tell you what's actually wrong.

## What you'll have at the end of 8 weeks

- 8 weekly repos demonstrating distinct skills (raw API, RAG, ReAct, LangGraph + MCP, LoRA fine-tune, eval suite, capstone, polish).
- One capstone system worth demoing to a hiring manager — a multi-step agent with structured outputs, RAG, MCP tools, evals and observability.
- A 1500-word architecture writeup describing the capstone's tradeoffs and what "10× scale" would change.
- Resume bullets you can actually defend in interviews ("reduced RAG p95 latency from 800ms to 180ms via hybrid search + reranking").
